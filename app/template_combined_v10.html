Why it ships at zero

The end-pull ships at zero, and that is a finding rather than a preference. Filtering the artefacts out leaves no genuine terminus within reach of the belt at all: of the twenty this found, fourteen were fragments of one to three cells — at 92.6 m a cell an A-road leaves specks wherever it thins, and every speck read as a terminus — and the six real ones are all 40 to 52 km away, on the London fringe. A motorway does not stop in open country; it only looks as though it does where the sheet clips it. A standing pull out there grew a detached blob of mould far outside the belt, which is exactly what it should not do.

Meeting the existing network is

Meeting the existing network is done by the switch above instead: trunk and A-road ground is cheaper to travel, so a route that finds one takes it up and ends up sharing its alignment. That is what finds the nearest main road, and unlike a standing food source it cannot pull anything out of the corridor, because it only rewards a route that was going past anyway.

Why meet the existing roads

A new road that ignores every road already on the ground is a diagram, not a plan. The trunk and A/B roads from Ordnance Survey are better ground to travel on, so a route tends to take one up; and where one of them ends inside the sheet there is a standing pull, so the new network meets the old at a junction rather than crossing it.

Roads that bound no cell

Where the mould reached out into open country along a single strand and never looped back, there is no ring and so no cell — the ground reads as dark but nothing encloses it. Those roads are drawn dashed. On this run 151 km of 1,117 bound nothing, off 107 loose ends. It is not an error to be tidied away: a road that serves land without enclosing a block is a real thing on a plan. If you want cells there, the mould has to mesh there — that is a change to the run, not to the extraction.

Editing by hand

Click a cell to select it, shift-click to add more. Space joins them into one, M then a click merges one cell into the neighbour you click, K keeps a cell (the floor and the automatic merges leave it alone), Delete removes one (it stays road), Ctrl+Z undoes, Esc cancels. A joined or merged cell is kept as you made it. Every hand edit is stored against the road ids and put back each time you Extract this network, or move a slider that extracts again; a new network clears them. Joining takes out the roads between the chosen cells and reads the faces again, so the result is still one closed polyline.

Why the outer edge is smoothed on its own

The roads round the outside of the network have cells on one side only, so they are smoothed much harder than the roads two cells share. Bends shorter than this are taken out; bends twice as long are cut to about a third. Real junctions stay exactly where they are; the corners a merge left behind are released first. Each smoothed road is checked against every other road — no crossing, no junction swept over, the order of roads at each junction kept — and one that fails is tried at a half and a quarter, else left as it was. 0 is off.

Why the road is smoothed, not the ring

Both of these now smooth the road, once, before the parcels are read off it — so two neighbours are handed the same polyline rather than each smoothing its own copy of it. That is what stops them crossing. Every parcel closes exactly — first point and last point the same point — because a ring that merely ends near where it began is not a closed polyline and Rhino will say so.

How the smoothing works

The one that takes the kinks out. The ring is resampled evenly and then averaged: a spike is by definition the point furthest from the average of its neighbours, so it is the point this moves most, and a few passes remove it. Corner-cutting and splines cannot — they take the same fraction off a gentle bend and a reversal alike, which is why the kinks kept coming back. Every point is held inside the face it came from while it smooths, so a cell can never cross its neighbour and never takes road land.

Which way to fewer cells

Two ways to get a dozen big cells rather than fifty small ones, and they are different questions. Aim for about keeps giving the smallest cell away to the neighbour it shares the most road with until the count comes down — it does not care where they are. One cell per node does: every cell is claimed by the station it can reach across the fewest roads, so what comes out is each node’s own territory along the spine. Both work by taking roads OUT and reading the faces again, so the result is still one closed ring each and not several glued along a seam.

What a finger is

A finger is where a cell narrows to a neck and then reaches on past it — a pimple on one cell and a notch on the other. No corner test sees it, because every turn on the way out can be gentle; what is wrong is the doubling back. With the switch on, any neck narrower than the figure set here, reaching on at least as far as the second figure, is cut with a new straight road across the neck, and the piece joins the cell it lies against, so nothing is left over as empty ground. A finger that sticks out of the network’s outer edge with no cell beside it is trimmed back to the new road. A finger is only cut when it is under a third of its cell and what remains is still above the smallest cell, so a genuinely U-shaped cell keeps its arms. The new roads are drawn dashed and go into the DXF with the other roads. With the switch off, the old ring cut runs instead, which only removes ground. Either way the short hairpins at junctions are taken off the ring.

Kerb, not centreline

A parcel is a face of the centreline graph, so left alone it owns half the carriageway on each side. At the kerb it is pulled in by half the road width instead — the land the road wraps around, which is the rule this app was built on.

Parcel curve is the one

Parcel curve is the one that softens them. Because the road is really a double line, the two sides of it need not be the same curve, so each parcel is simplified to this tolerance and then a real spline is drawn through what is left — a curve, not a polygon with its corners shaved off. Higher is looser and softer; lower holds tighter to where the mould put the boundary. It is safe at any setting because every point is then held half a road width off every centreline: two neighbours sit on opposite sides of the same line, so they are a full road apart and cannot cross. Where a parcel relaxes away, the road widens there; where it tries to bulge in, it stops at the kerb.

Threading a cell

For the wool thread

Select cells on the drawing, then Choose selected. A chosen cell is green and carries what the wool thread needs: its spine, which is the railway clipped to it, and either a centre or a corridor.

Centre, or corridor

Centre, or corridor. Where a cell has a station, that station is the centre and the threads converge on it. Where it has none there is nothing to converge on, so the spine inside the cell is offset into a green corridor instead, divided equally into anchor points, and each anchor connects to both north and south — which is the shape the wool app already has, its two gateway groups being named Road north and Road south.

What road view shows

Road view answers a different question: what is happening inside the cells. Nothing is filled — the cell is only its outline — and the roads are ranked by weight. Motorway and trunk are boldest and keep their own colour; A and B keep theirs; existing local roads go grey, because they are context rather than the subject; and the mould's new roads are drawn heavier than anything the wool thread will lay inside a cell, so the two read apart at a glance.
